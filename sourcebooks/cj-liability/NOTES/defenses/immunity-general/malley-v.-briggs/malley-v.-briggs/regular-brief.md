# Regular Brief

## _Malley v. Briggs_, 475 U.S. 335 (1986)

## Relevant Facts

Edward Malley, a Rhode Island state trooper, was investigating Paul Driscoll for drug-related activities. Based on intercepted phone calls, Malley believed a party involving drugs took place at the home of the respondents (Briggs et al.). Malley prepared felony complaints and supporting affidavits for the arrest of the respondents, which a judge approved, leading to their arrest. The charges were later dropped as the grand jury did not return an indictment.

## Issues

What is the **degree of immunity is accorded** a defendant police officer in a damages action under 42 U.S. C. § 1983 when it is alleged that the officer caused the plaintiffs to be unconstitutionally arrested **by presenting a judge with a complaint and a supporting affidavit which failed to establish probable cause**?

**Case-specific:** whether a reasonably well-trained officer in petitioner's position would have known that his affidavit failed to establish probable cause and that he should not have applied for the warrant

## Holding

**None**.

* Neither absolute nor qualified immunity exists **where the warrant application is so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render the belief in its existence unreasonable**. This officer (petitioner) is not entitled to either absolute or qualified immunity.
* An officer's **good-faith belief that probable cause exists is insufficient to justify immunity**; whether a judge signs the warrant is irrelevant to whether an officer is entitled to immunity.

**Yes**, a reasonably well-trained officer in petitioner's position would have known that his affidavit failed to establish probable cause and that he should not have applied for the warrant

## Rules

* **Qualified immunity** (fundamentally intertwined with the defense of "**good faith**") from personal liability is the norm for executive officers, generally.
  * At common law, **police officers have qualified immunity (from personal liability) for false arrests when sued under § 1983**.
*   **Public Policy**

    * For a state officer to have absolute immunity from personal liability for constitutional violations must show that public policy favors absolute (versus qualified immunity).



    * In the criminal procedural context, **absolute immunity relating to § 1983 suits requires that the subject's function or actions be intimately associated with the judicial phase of the process**. The point <mark style="color:red;">(i.e., the public policy)</mark> is that without absolute immunity, the integrity of the judicial process would be impaired.&#x20;



    * **Immunity furthers a goal avoiding excessive disruption of government and permitting the resolution of many insubstantial claims** on summary judgment.



    * **Qualified immunity:**
      * **good-faith** inquiry is confined to the objectively ascertainable question whether a reasonably well-trained officer would have known that the search was illegal despite the magistrate's authorization
      * **will not apply** (thus, there is no immunity) **when no reasonably competent officer could have concluded that a warrant should issue; but if officers of reasonable competence could disagree, there should be immunity.**

## Analysis

* **No absolute immunity: Officers** applying for warrants and performing other law enforcement and peacekeeping functions are **not intimately associated with the judicial process** (fundamentally, prosecutors initiate the process, so they have absolute immunity)
  * Public policy does not favor absolute immunity or qualified for officers applying for warrants if **they knowingly violate clearly established law or are plainly incompetent**.&#x20;
    * Absolute Test: Objective reasonableness (versus subjective beliefs) furthers the public policy goal of avoiding excessive disruption of government and permitting the resolution of many insubstantial claims on summary judgment.
    * **Qualified Test: protection for all but the (1) plainly incompetent or (2) knowing violators of the law**
      * Therefore: Objectively, **if no reasonably competent officer would have concluded that probable cause exists to support a warrant, then the officer should not receive immunity**. If reasonable minds could differ, however, the officer should receive immunity.

<summary><mark style="color:red;">Notes about the previous bullet</mark></summary>

<mark style="color:red;">First, although the court doesn't expressly state what "perform\[ing] as a magistrate should" means, it is referring to the "docket pressures" being such that limited time frames may impede the judge's ability to thoroughly assess probable cause from the warrant application.</mark>&#x20;

<mark style="color:red;">Second, pragmatically, the first time a reasonably thorough assessment of probable cause can be performed will be at the preliminary hearing. Although probable cause is evaluated at the defendant's initial appearance, this determination is perfunctory and based largely on the information that law enforcement has communicated to the prosecutor. So, the probable cause determination at the initial appearance is not realistic a "safety valve" for a defendant's unconstitutional detention.</mark>
