&#x20;F:

&#x20;

Richards invested for being in a ring of people dealing drugs out of motel rooms in Madison

&#x20;

Madison police requested a search warrant that would give authorization for a “no-knock” entry into Richards' motel room; the Magistrate explicitly deleted those portions of the warrant

&#x20;

Officers get to the motel room at 3:40 a.m. One officer (Officer Pharo) is dressed as a maintenance worker, and has a team of several plainclothes officers (and at least one person in uniform).

&#x20;

Officer Pharo knocked on the door, Richards asks who it is, and Pharo said he was a maintenance man.

&#x20;

With the chain still on the door, Richards cracked it open; he did see someone in a uniform behind Pharo, and slammed the door closed.

&#x20;

After waiting two or three seconds, the officers began kicking and ramming the door open.

&#x20;

At trial, the officers testified that they identified themselves as police while they were kicking the door in. When they finally did break into the room, the officers caught Richards trying to escape through the window. They also found cash and cocaine hidden in plastic bags above the bathroom ceiling tiles.

&#x20;

The trial court found that forcibly entering prior to knocking and announcing was justified because Richards' "strange behavior" could imply that he might try to destroy evidence or escape

&#x20;

WI Supreme Court agreed, also saying that officers are never required to knock and announce when executing a search warrant in a felony drug investigation



I:

Did the Wisconsin Supreme Court err in creating a **blanket exception** to the knock-and-announce requirement of the Fourth Amendment for all felony drug investigations?&#x20;

Did the officers' actions in entering Richards' motel room **without** knocking and announcing violate the Fourth Amendment under the specific circumstances of the case?



H:&#x20;

No blanket exception to the knock and announce principle; but

the officers' no-knock entry into Richards' motel room did not violate the Fourth Amendment

&#x20;R

* Although felony drug investigations can be accompanied by circumstances that can present a threat of violate or destruction of evidence with advanced notice, there **can't be exceptions solely based on the "general category of criminal behavior"** involved.
  * e.g., not every drug investigation presents the same level of risk to officer safety or the potential for evidence destruction. For instance, the occupants might not be involved in drug activity, or some evidence or contraband might not be easily disposable
  * blanket exceptions may make the rule meaningless
* &#x20;Whether no-knock entry is justified is a case-by-case determination:
  * When police have **reasonable suspicion** that knocking and announcing their presence, under the particular circumstances, would be dangerous or futile, or that it would inhibit the effective investigation of the crime
* Here, reasonable suspicion existed:
  * Richards' "strange behavior" of opening the door, seeing a uniformed officer, and immediately slamming it shut. This behavior indicated he likely knew it was the police. Given this awareness and the easily disposable nature of drugs, the officers' decision to force entry without further announcement was reasonable
