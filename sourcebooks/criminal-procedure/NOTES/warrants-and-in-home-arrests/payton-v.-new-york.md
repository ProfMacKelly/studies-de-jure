# Payton v. New York

## Facts

New York detectives had assembled evidence sufficient for PC to believe that **Payton had murdered the manager of a gas station** two days earlier.

&#x20;At about 7:30 a.m. on January 15, **six officers went to Payton’s apartment in the Bronx, intending to arrest him. They had not obtained a warrant.**

Although **light and music emanated from the apartment, there was no response to their knock on the metal door. They summoned emergency assistance and, about 30 minutes later, used crowbars to break open the door and enter the apartment. No one was there.**

In pl**ain view, however, was a .30-caliber shell casing that was seized and la**ter admitted into evidence at Payton’s murder trial.

New York statutes **authorizing police officers to enter a private residence without a warrant and with force, if necessary, to make a routine felony arrest**

(in the companion case, police broke into Obie Riddick's home to arrest him for robbery, again without a warrant but with probable cause.)

## Issue

**Constitutionality of New York statutes authorizing police officers to enter a private residence without a warrant and with force, if necessary, to make a routine felony arrest**.

(i.e., whether the Fourth Amendment prohibits police from making a warrantless and nonconsensual entry into a suspect's home to make a routine felony arrest)

## Holding

**Not constitutional unless there are exigent circumstances**

## **Rules**

Default rule of 4th Amend: Unreasonable searches & seizures prohibit, and warrants are required

* 4th Amend applies "to all invasions on the part of the government and its employees of the sanctity of a man’s home and the privacies of life.”
* Applies to both people and property
* The home has the highest degree of protected privacy interests
  * “physical entry of the home is the chief evil against which the wording of the Fourth Amendment is directed.”
* Thus, warrantless searches and seizures inside a home are presumptively unreasonable
  * Exigent circumstances required for warrantless entry
* An arrest warrant founded on probable cause implicitly carries with it the limited authority to enter a dwelling in which the suspect lives when there is reason to believe the suspect is within

Plain View exception:

* Not invasion of privacy with weapons or contraband in public place or otherwise plainly identifiable as evidence or contraband, _as long as the officer sees it while in a location that is constitutionally permissible_

## &#x20;Analysis

* long-standing principle that a person's home is a private domain and generally not subject to intrusion without a warrant: "physical entry of the home is the chief evil against which the wording of the Fourth Amendment is directed."
  * arrests in a public place, which can be made on probable cause, are thus distinct from an arrest in a home, which requires a warrant.
* Absent exigent circumstances, the threshold or the home's entrance may not be crossed reasonably without a warrant.  &#x20;
  * exigent circs: immediate action is necessary to prevent imminent danger, the destruction of evidence, or the escape of a suspect
    * **\[BUT** the court did not address whether exig circs existed because it was never an issue addressed by the lower courts; the NY Ct Appls described as "routine" with "ample time to obtain a warran&#x74;**]**
* The Court rejected the argument that a warrantless entry to arrest is reasonable simply because there is probable cause to believe the suspect committed a felony.
* Although the New York statutes in question authorized warrantless home arrests, state law cannot override the protections of the Fourth Amendment (i.e., states cannot provide less protection than the 4th Amendment)

## &#x20;Dissent

Rehnquist:

Claims that the majority's reasoning is consistent with historical practices and the majority's rule is too inflexible

Claims that the majority is incorrectly treating arrests in the home as inherently more intrusive than other arrests

The existing restrictions of the NY law was enough (1) felony; (2) knock-and-announce requirement; (3) daytime only; and (4) probable cause -- because these protect against abusive or arbitrary practices by law enforcement

Claims the majority's inflexible rule will hamper law enforcement due to potential of a delayed arrest due to needing to get a warrant

Exigent circumstances can be difficult to decide and must be made quickly

Essentially, Rehnquist has issue over the language "routine" w/ "felony arrests"
