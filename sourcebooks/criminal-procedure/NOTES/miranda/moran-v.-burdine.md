**Case Name:** _John Moran v. Brian K. Burbine_ **Citation:** 475 U.S. 412 (1986) (Note: The sources provided confirm the case name and year but do not include the specific U.S. Reports volume and page number, which is included here for completeness based on common knowledge of the case.) **Decided:** 1986 (Note: The specific date in 1986 is not provided in the sources.) **Opinion By:** Justice O’CONNOR **Dissent By:** Justice STEVENS, joined by Justice BRENNAN and Justice MARSHALL

**Facts:** The respondent, Brian Burbine, confessed to the murder of Mary Jo Hickey. Several months after the victim's death, Burbine and two others were arrested for a local burglary in Cranston, Rhode Island. A detective learned from an informant that the murder suspect, known as "Butch," lived at a certain address; Burbine lived there and was known by that name. After being informed of his _Miranda_ rights, Burbine refused to execute a written waiver regarding the burglary charge.

Police obtained statements from the other suspects that implicated Burbine in the murder. Providence police were contacted and came to the Cranston station to question Burbine about the murder.

That evening, around 7:45 p.m., Burbine's sister called the Public Defender’s Office to get legal help for him regarding the breaking and entering charge; she was unaware he was a murder suspect. An Assistant Public Defender, Ms. Munson, was contacted.

At 8:15 p.m., Ms. Munson called the Cranston police station's detective division. She asked if Brian Burbine was held (yes) and explained she would act as his legal counsel if police intended to put him in a lineup or question him. An unidentified male voice told her the police would _not_ be questioning Burbine or putting him in a lineup and were "through with him for the night". Ms. Munson was not told that Providence police were present or that Burbine was a murder suspect.

At all relevant times, **Burbine was unaware of his sister's attempts to retain counsel and of Ms. Munson's telephone conversation. Less than an hour after Ms. Munson's call, Providence police began questioning Burbine about the murder. Prior to each interrogation session, Burbine was informed of his&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights. On three separate occasions, he signed a written form acknowledging he understood his right to the presence of an attorney and explicitly stating that he "\[did] not want an attorney called or appointed for \[him]" before giving a statement. Burbine had access to a telephone but apparently did not use it. He eventually signed three written statements fully admitting to the murder.**

**Procedural History:** Prior to trial, Burbine moved to suppress the confessions. The trial court denied the motion, finding that he had received _Miranda_ warnings, understood his rights, and had **"knowingly, intelligently, and voluntarily waived his privilege against self-incrimination \[and] his right to counsel"**. The court acknowledged Ms. Munson's call but found no police "conspiracy or collusion". It held that the right to counsel belongs solely to the defendant and cannot be asserted by a lawyer, and since Burbine never asked for a lawyer, the call was irrelevant to the waiver's validity.

The jury found Burbine guilty of first-degree murder. The Supreme Court of Rhode Island affirmed the conviction, holding that the failure to inform Burbine of Ms. Munson's efforts did not undermine the waiver's validity, nor did _Miranda_ or any other decision require police to honor Ms. Munson's request not to question him. The state court noted the separate police departments as supporting the finding of no collusion and reiterated that the right to counsel belongs solely to the accused, not "benign third parties".

Burbine sought habeas corpus in the U.S. District Court, which was denied. The Court of Appeals for the First Circuit reversed, holding that the police's conduct (misleading the attorney and failing to inform Burbine) had "fatally tainted" his otherwise valid waiver of his Fifth Amendment rights. The Court of Appeals reasoned that police had deprived Burbine of information "crucial to his ability to waive his rights knowingly and intelligently" and found their conduct to be "deliberate or reckless irresponsibility". This "blameworthy action," combined with Burbine's ignorance of the call, "vitiate\[d] any claim that \[the] waiver of counsel was knowing and voluntary".

The Supreme Court of the United States granted certiorari.

**Issue:** **Did police conduct—specifically, failing to inform a suspect of an attorney's efforts to reach him or misleading the attorney—invalidate an otherwise voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights?**

**Holding:** **No. The police's failure to inform the suspect of an attorney's efforts to reach him or their misleading statements to the attorney did not invalidate an otherwise voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights.** The Federal Constitution did not require the exclusion of Burbine's confessions.

**Reasoning:** The Court found that Burbine's waiver was valid because he **was fully informed of his&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights, understood them, and voluntarily chose to waive them**. The inquiry into waiver has two dimensions: **voluntariness (free choice, no coercion)** **and knowing/intelligent waiver (full awareness of the right's nature and consequences).** The Court found both present: the waiver was voluntary (no pressure, Burbine may have initiated contact) and knowing/intelligent regarding the standard _Miranda_ warnings.

The crucial point for the Court was that **events occurring outside the suspect's presence and unknown to him cannot affect his capacity to comprehend and knowingly relinquish a constitutional right**. **While the information about the attorney's call might have been "useful" and perhaps influenced his decision,** the Constitution does not require police to provide information to help a suspect "calibrate his self-interest". Once a suspect's decision not to rely on his rights is uncoerced, he knew he could remain silent and request counsel, and was aware his statements could be used against him, the waiver is valid.

The Court stated that the police's state of mind or culpability in failing to inform Burbine was irrelevant to the validity of the waiver. Even deliberate deception of an attorney could not affect the suspect's decision _unless_ he was aware of it. The failure to inform Burbine was not the kind of "trickery" that invalidates a waiver, because it **did not deprive him of knowledge&#x20;**_**essential**_**&#x20;to understanding the nature of his rights and the consequences of abandoning them.**

The Court declined to extend _Miranda_ to require police to inform a suspect of an attorney's efforts. _Miranda_ warnings are prophylactic measures to protect the right against compulsory self-incrimination, not constitutional rights themselves. Their purpose is to dissipate the compulsion of custodial interrogation, not to regulate police conduct unrelated to that compulsion. A rule focusing on how police treat an attorney (conduct unknown to the suspect) is irrelevant to the compulsion experienced by the suspect. While such a rule might "marginally" add to _Miranda_'s goal, t**he Court found overriding practical considerations against it and believed it would upset the "subtle balance"&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;struck between law enforcement needs (securing confessions is "essential") and protecting Fifth Amendment rights**. Full comprehension of _Miranda_ rights is sufficient to dispel coercion, and requiring police to inform the suspect of an attorney's call would only provide a minimal benefit to the privilege while imposing a "substantial cost" on securing admissions. The Court noted this additional information's likely effect would be to convince the suspect _not_ to speak, which is not required by _Miranda_.

The Court also briefly rejected arguments based on the Sixth Amendment (because adversarial judicial proceedings had not yet formally begun) and the Due Process Clause (because the police conduct, while potentially unethical, did not "shock the sensibilities of civilized society").

**Judgment:** The judgment of the Court of Appeals was reversed, and the case was remanded for proceedings consistent with the Supreme Court's opinion.

**Dissent:** Justice Stevens, joined by Justices Brennan and Marshall, dissented, viewing the decision as a "startling departure" from the fundamental principles of an accusatorial, not inquisitorial, system of justice. The dissent argued that the decision ignored carefully reasoned state court decisions, violated American Bar Association Standards, and **misapprehended the central principles of this Court's decisions on incommunicado interrogation.** The dissent **framed the issue around the role of the lawyer in society – as an aid to understanding constitutional rights in an accusatorial system, not a "nettlesome obstacle**". They believed the Court's decision, like the police conduct, prioritized punishing a criminal but "trampled on well-established legal principles and flouted the spirit of our accusatorial system of justice".
