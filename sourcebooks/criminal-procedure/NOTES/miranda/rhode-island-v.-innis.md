# Rhode Island v. Innis

Here is a detailed case brief for _Rhode Island v. Innis_, drawing on the provided sources:

**Case Name:** Rhode Island v. Thomas J. Innis **Court:** Supreme Court of the United States **Year:** 1980

**Facts:** On January 12, 1975, a taxicab driver, John Mulvaney, disappeared and was found four days later buried in a shallow grave, having died from a shotgun blast. On January 17, 1975, another taxicab driver, Gerald Aubin, reported being robbed by a man with a sawed-off shotgun. Aubin identified a picture of the respondent, Thomas J. Innis, as the assailant. Providence police began searching the Mount Pleasant area. Around 4:30 a.m. on the same day, Patrolman Lovell **spotted and arrested Innis, who was unarmed. Patrolman Lovell advised Innis of his&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights. While waiting for other officers, Lovell did not converse with Innis except to respond to Innis's request for a cigarette**. **Sergeant Sears and Captain Leyden arrived and also gave Innis&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;warnings. Innis stated that he understood his rights and wanted to speak with a lawyer**. Captain Leyden directed that Innis be transported to the central police station in a caged wagon. Three officers, Patrolmen Gleckman, Williams, and McKenna, were assigned to accompany Innis, and **Captain Leyden instructed them not to question, intimidate, or coerce him.**

While en route, **Patrolman Gleckman initiated a conversation with Patrolman McKenna concerning the missing shotgun. Gleckman stated that he frequented the area near a school for handicapped children and was concerned that a child might find the weapon with shells and hurt themselves. Patrolman McKenna concurred, stating it was a safety factor and they should continue searching for the weapon.** Patrolman Williams overheard this conversation. **Innis then interrupted the conversation, stating the officers should turn the car around so he could show them where the gun was located. They had traveled no more than a mile, a trip of only a few minutes.**

The police vehicle returned to the scene of the arrest. **Captain Leyden again advised Innis of his&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights. Innis replied he understood but wanted to get the gun out of the way because of the kids in the area. Innis then led police to a nearby field and pointed out the shotgun under some rocks.**

Innis was indicted for kidnaping, robbery, and murder. He moved to suppress the shotgun and his statements. The trial judge found Innis was repeatedly advised of his rights, the officers' concern was understandable, and Innis's decision was an intelligent waiver of his _Miranda_ right to remain silent. The trial court admitted the evidence, and Innis was found guilty.

The Rhode Island Supreme Court set aside the conviction, concluding Innis invoked his right to counsel and was "interrogated" by the officers in the vehicle without a valid waiver. The state court viewed the officers' conversation as "subtle coercion" equivalent to "interrogation" under _Miranda_, finding insufficient evidence of waiver. The state court held the evidence was obtained in violation of _Miranda_ standards and ordered a new trial. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to address the meaning of "interrogation" under _Miranda v. Arizona_.

**Issue:** **Whether the respondent was “interrogated” in violation of the standards promulgated in the&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;opinion, specifically defining the term "interrogation" under&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**.**

**Rule of Law: Under&#x20;**_**Miranda v. Arizona**_**, once a defendant in custody asks to speak with a lawyer, all interrogation must cease until an attorney is present. If an individual states they want an attorney before speaking, police must respect their decision to remain silent. The special procedural safeguards outlined in&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;are required where a suspect in custody is subjected to interrogation.**

**Holding:** The respondent **was not “interrogated”** within the meaning of _Miranda_. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Rhode Island is vacated, and the case is remanded.

**Reasoning (Majority Opinion - Stewart, J.):** The Court **defined "interrogation"** under _Miranda_, acknowledging that the original opinion referred to **"questioning initiated by law enforcement officers".** However, the Court did not construe _Miranda_ so narrowly. The **core concern of&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;was the potential for the "interrogation environment" to "subjugate the individual to the will of his examiner**" **and undermine the privilege against compulsory self-incrimination, which could be achieved through police practices beyond express questioning, such as psychological ploys**.

The Court concluded that the _Miranda_ safeguards come into play whenever a person in custody is subjected to either **express questioning** or its **functional equivalent**. The **"functional equivalent" refers to any words or actions on the part of the police (other than those normally attendant to arrest and custody) that the police** **should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response from the suspect**. This definition focuses primarily on the **perceptions of the suspect** rather than the police's intent, as _Miranda_ aimed to protect suspects against coercive practices. However, p**olice are not held accountable for unforeseeable results;** the definition applies only to words or actions they _should have known_ were reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response.

Applying this definition to the facts, the Court found that Innis was not interrogated. The conversation between Patrolmen Gleckman and McKenna **did not involve express questioning of Innis; it was a dialogue between the officers to which no response was invited**. The Court also found the conversation was **not the "functional equivalent" of questioning. The officers "should not have known" that their conversation was reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response from Innis. There was nothing in the record to suggest the officers knew Innis was particularly susceptible to an appeal concerning handicapped children's safety or that he was unusually disoriented**. Given that the conversation consisted of "**a few off hand remarks,"** the officers should not have known it was reasonably likely that Innis would respond. This was not a lengthy harangue, and the comments were not deemed "particularly evocative" under the circumstances. The Court held that the Rhode Island Supreme Court erred in equating "subtle compulsion" with interrogation. While the comments may have struck a responsive chord, leading to "subtle compulsion," an incriminating response must be the product of words or actions the police should have known were reasonably likely to elicit such a response, which was not established in this case.

**Dissent (Marshall, J., joined by Brennan, J.):** Justice Marshall agreed with the Court's definition of "interrogation" but disagreed with its application to the facts. He emphasized the context: Innis was arrested, handcuffed, advised of rights multiple times, and placed in close quarters in a police car with three officers. The conversation about the shotgun and the risk to handicapped children began almost immediately. He argued that Gleckman's statements were not mere "off hand remarks" but conveyed a simple, powerful message about preventing a child's death, constituting an extremely **strong appeal to&#x20;**_**any**_**&#x20;suspect's conscienc**e. **Appealing to a suspect to confess for the sake of others is a classic** interrogation technique. The result should not differ because the remarks were nominally addressed to McKenna; the officers were "**talking back and forth" in close quarters, knew Innis would hear, and were responsible for the pressure they created.** This was not a case of accidentally overheard conversation. Justice Marshall believed the decision was an "aberration".

**Dissent (Stevens, J.):** Justice Stevens believed the state court's conclusion of interrogation rested on a proper interpretation of facts and law. He argued that to fully protect a suspect's right to be free from interrogation, **the definition of "interrogation" must include any police statement or conduct with the same&#x20;**_**purpose or effect**_**&#x20;as a direct question, including those that&#x20;**_**appear**_**&#x20;or are&#x20;**_**designed**_**&#x20;to call for a response.** He contended the Court's test ("reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response") provides less protection and will likely exclude statements without question marks. Using examples of how the officers' concern could have been voiced, **he argued that statements designed to elicit a response should be interrogation, regardless of whether the police&#x20;**_**should have known**_**&#x20;they were likely to succeed with a specific suspect.** He suggested the Court's test **incentivizes police to ignore invoked rights and use psychological pressure** while avoiding direct questions, creating a "trap" for suspects.

**Significance:** _Rhode Island v. Innis_ is significant for defining "interrogation" under _Miranda_ to include not just express questioning but also the "functional equivalent," which consists of words or actions by police that they _should know_ are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response, focusing on the suspect's perceptions.
