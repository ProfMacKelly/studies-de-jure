# North Carolina v. Butler

Here is a detailed case brief for _North Carolina v. Butler_, drawing on the provided sources:

**Case Name:** _North Carolina v. Willie Thomas Butler_ **Citation:** 441 U.S. 369 (1979) **Decided:** April 24, 1979 **Opinion By:** Mr. Justice Stewart **Dissent By:** Mr. Justice Brennan, joined by Mr. Justice Marshall and Mr. Justice Stevens

**Facts:** The respondent, Willie Thomas Butler, was convicted in a North Carolina trial court of kidnaping, armed robbery, and felonious assault. The evidence showed that he and another man robbed a gas station in Goldsboro, N.C., in December 1976, and shot the attendant, who survived but was paralyzed and testified against Butler. Butler was arrested by FBI agents in the Bronx, N.Y., based on a North Carolina fugitive warrant. FBI Agent Martinez testified that Butler was fully advised of his rights under the _Miranda_ case at the time of arrest. Butler was then taken to the FBI office in New Rochelle, N.Y.. Agents determined that Butler had an 11th-grade education and was literate, and he was given the Bureau's "Advice of Rights" form, which he read. **When asked if he understood his rights, he stated that he did**. **However, Butler refused to sign the waiver at the bottom of the form**. He was told he did not have to speak or sign the form but that the agents would like him to talk to them. **Butler replied: "I will talk to you but I am not signing any form"**. **He then made inculpatory statements**. Agent Martinez testified that Butler **said nothing when advised of his right to the assistance of a lawyer, and Butler never requested counsel or attempted to terminate the questioning**.

**Procedural History:** At trial, Butler moved to suppress the incriminating statements, arguing he had not waived his right to the assistance of counsel. The trial court denied the motion, finding that Butler's statement was made freely and voluntarily after being advised of his _Miranda_ rights, including the right to an attorney, that he understood his rights, and that he **effectively waived his rights, including the right to have an attorney present, by indicating his willingness to answer questions** after reading the rights form. Butler's statements were admitted, and the jury convicted him of each offense. On appeal, the North Carolina Supreme Court reversed the convictions and ordered a new trial. It found the statements were admitted in violation of _Miranda_ requirements because Butler had refused to waive his rights in writing and there had not been a _specific_ oral waiver. The Supreme Court of the United States granted certiorari to consider whether the North Carolina Supreme Court's _per se_ rule (requiring explicit waiver) properly understood the _Miranda_ decision.

**Issue:** **Did&#x20;**_**Miranda v. Arizona**_**&#x20;establish a&#x20;**_**per se**_**&#x20;rule requiring an express written or oral waiver of the right to counsel as a condition for admitting statements made by a person under custodial interrogation, or can waiver be inferred from the defendant's actions and words?**

**Holding:** The Supreme Court held that **an express statement of waiver is not indispensable to a finding that a defendant waived their&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;rights**. **Waiver can be clearly inferred from the actions and words of the person interrogated, coupled with an understanding of their rights**. The North Carolina Supreme Court erred in requiring an explicit waiver.

**Reasoning:** The Court stated that _Miranda_ held an express statement could constitute a waiver and that silence alone could not, but it did _not_ hold that an express statement was indispensabl&#x65;**. The question of waiver is not one of form, but rather whether the defendant in fact knowingly and voluntarily waived the rights delineated in&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**. While mere silence is not enough, silence coupled with an understanding of rights and a course of conduct indicating waiver&#x20;**_**may**_**&#x20;support a conclusion of waiver in some cases. Courts must presume non-waiver, and the prosecution bears a great burden, but waiver can be clearly inferred from actions and words.**

The **purpose of&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**'s prophylactic rules is to combat the inherently compelling pressures of custodial interrogation and ensure the privilege against self-incrimination is exercised**. The Court reasoned that the North Carolina Supreme Court's _per se_ rule does not speak to these concerns, as Butler was adequately and effectively apprised of his rights. The only question was the waiver of the right to counsel. The Court found no reason why an _express_ waiver must be necessary in the absence of evidence to the contrary, noting that determining waiver often depends on the particular facts and circumstances of the case, including the background, experience, and conduct of the accused, even for rights as fundamental as the right to counsel at trial.

The Court saw no reason to discard this standard (determining waiver based on particular facts and circumstances) for an inflexible _per se_ rule. It noted that it appeared that every other court that had considered the question had reached the same conclusion: Ten of the eleven U.S. Courts of Appeals and courts in at least 17 States had held that an explicit statement is not invariably necessary. By creating an inflexible rule that no implicit waiver can ever suffice, the North Carolina Supreme Court went beyond the requirements of federal law, and a state court cannot add to or subtract from the mandates of the U.S. Constitution.

**Judgment:** The judgment of the North Carolina Supreme Court was vacated, and the case was remanded for further proceedings not inconsistent with the U.S. Supreme Court's opinion.

**Dissent:** Mr. Justice Brennan, joined by Mr. Justice Marshall and Mr. Justice Stevens, dissented. They argued that the majority's rule, **allowing waiver to be inferred from "actions and words," shrouds the question of waiver in "half-light," permitting courts to construct inferences from ambiguous words and gestures.** The dissent contended that the very premise of _Miranda_ - recognizing the compulsion inherent in custodial interrogation - requires that ambiguity be interpreted _against_ the interrogator. Under such compelling conditions, only the most explicit waivers should be considered knowingly and freely given.

The dissent highlighted the instant case as a clear example of the need for an express waiver requirement. They noted disagreements over whether Butler was orally advised of his rights and disputes over his literacy, which the majority deemed resolved by his receipt of a written form. The dissent argued that if Butler could not read or was not read his rights, he could not have knowingly waived them. Furthermore, even if he could read, his **refusal to sign a written waiver form suggested that his subsequent oral statements were not intended to signify relinquishment of his rights**.

The dissent predicted that allowing inferences from uncertain meaning ("actions and words") would lead to inconsistent judicial findings, potentially resulting in violations of defendants' rights or frustrating effective law enforcement. They argued that a simple prophylactic r**ule requiring the police to obtain an express waiver of the right to counsel before interrogation eliminates these difficulties and would not impose a new burden on the police, but merely make the existing burden of obtaining&#x20;**_**some**_**&#x20;kind of waiver explicit**. The dissent concluded that if Agent Martinez had simply asked Butler a clear question like, "Do you waive your right to a lawyer?", the journey through three courts would have been unnecessary.
