# JDB v. North Carolina

Okay, here is a detailed case brief for _J.D.B. v. North Carolina_, based on the provided sources:

**J.D.B. v. North Carolina** **Citation:** 564 U.S. 261 (2011) (Citation not explicitly provided in sources, but this is the standard citation for the case) **Court:** Supreme Court of the United States

**Facts:**

* Petitioner J.D.B. was a **13-year-old**, seventh-grade student.
* He was questioned by police regarding two home break-ins and stolen items.
* Five days prior to the main questioning, police had stopped and questioned J.D.B. after seeing him in the neighborhood where the crimes occurred, and also spoke with his grandmother (legal guardian) and aunt that same day.
* Police later learned that a digital camera matching the description of a stolen item was found at J.D.B.'s middle school and had been seen in his possession.
* Investigator DiCostanzo, the juvenile investigator assigned to the case, went to J.D.B.'s school to question him.
* **Upon arrival, DiCostanzo informed the school resource officer (a uniformed police officer on detail to the school), the assistant principal, and an administrative intern of his purpose**.
* Although DiCostanzo asked school administrators to verify J.D.B.'s information, n**either police nor administrators contacted J.D.B.'s grandmother before questioning began**.
* **J.D.B. was removed from his social studies classroom by the uniformed school resource officer and escorted to a conference room.**
* **In the conference room, J.D.B. was met by DiCostanzo, the assistant principal, and the administrative intern. The door to the room was closed**.
* J.D.B. was questioned for **30 to 45 minutes with the two police officers and two administrators present.**
* Before questioning, J.D.B. was given **neither&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;warnings nor the opportunity to speak to his grandmother**.
* He was also **not informed that he was free to leave** the room.
* Questioning began with small talk. J.D.B. initially denied wrongdoing.
* DiCostanzo pressed for details, asked about a prior incident, and **confronted J.D.B. with the stolen camera**.
* The assistant principal urged **J.D.B. to "do the right thing," warning that "the truth always comes out in the end".**
* Eventually, J.D.B. asked if he would "still be in trouble" if he returned the "stuff".
* DiCostanzo responded that returning items would be helpful but "this thing is going to court" regardless.
* DiCostanzo then **warned that he might seek a secure custody order** if he believed J.D.B. would continue to break into homes.
* When J.D.B. aske**d what a secure custody order was, DiCostanzo explained it is where "you get sent to juvenile detention before cour**t".
* After learning of the prospect of juvenile detention, **J.D.B. confessed** that he and a friend were responsible.
* **Only after he confessed** did DiCostanzo inform J.D.B. that he could refuse to answer and was free to leave.
* J.D.B. nodded that he understood and provided more details, including the location of stolen items.
* He then wrote a statement at DiCostanzo's request.
* When the school day ended, J.D.B. was allowed to leave to catch his bus home.

**Procedural History:**

* Two juvenile petitions were filed against J.D.B..
* J.D.B.'s public defender moved to suppress his statements and resulting evidence, arguing he was interrogated in a custodial setting without _Miranda_ warnings and that his statements were involuntary.
* The trial court **denied the motion to suppress**, finding J.D.B. was not in custody at the school and his statements were voluntary.
* J.D.B. entered a transcript of admission to all four counts but renewed his objection to the suppression denial.
* The trial court adjudicated J.D.B. delinquent.
* A divided panel of the North Carolina Court of Appeals **affirmed**.
* The North Carolina Supreme Court, over two dissents, held that J.D.B. was **not in custody** when he confessed and declined "to extend the test for custody to include consideration of the age ... of an individual subjected to questioning by police".
* The Supreme Court of the United States **granted certiorari** to determine if a juvenile suspect's age is relevant to the _Miranda_ custody analysis.

**Issue:** I**s the age of a child subjected to police questioning relevant to the determination of whether the child was in "custody" for&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;purposes?**

**Holding:** **Yes**. The Supreme Court held that **a child's age properly informs the&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**&#x20;custody analysis**. The judgment of the North Carolina Supreme Court was **reversed**, and the case was remanded for state courts to reconsider the custody question taking J.D.B.'s age into account.

**Reasoning (Majority - Sotomayor, J.):**

* _Miranda_'s prophylactic measures are required only where there has been such a restriction on a person's freedom as to render him "in custody". **The custody analysis is an** **objective inquiry**.
* While the custody analysis is objective, **a child's age is relevant because it "would have affected how a reasonable person" in the suspect's position "would perceive his or her freedom to leave".**
* A **reasonable child** will sometimes **feel pressured to submit to police questioning when a reasonable adult would feel free to go.**
* Age is "more than a chronological fact"; it "**generates commonsense conclusions about behavior and perception" that are self-evident.**
* The Court has previously observed that children "**generally are less mature and responsible than adults," "lack the experience, perspective, and judgment" of adults, and "are more vulnerable or susceptible to ... outside pressures".**
* The law has historically reflected the understanding that children lack the capacity for mature judgment and full understanding of the world. Even in contexts using a "reasonable person" standard, like negligence suits, childhood is a relevant circumstance.
* Children "cannot be viewed simply as miniature adults".
* A child's age differs from other personal characteristics because it yields **objective conclusions** about susceptibility to influence and pressure. Considering age is not about determining a particular child's subjective mindset, but about how a reasonable child perceives their freedom.
* Ignoring age in the custody analysis for a juvenile suspect would make the inquiry "nonsensical" and "absurd," **forcing courts to evaluate the situation through the eyes of a reasonable adult subjected to circumstances specific to children**, such as being removed from a classroom by a school officer.
* The **coercive effect of the schoolhouse setting** cannot be separated from the fact that the person questioned is a student, whose presence is compulsory and disobedience grounds for discipline.
* As long as the child's age was known or objectively apparent to the officer, its inclusion in the custody analysis is consistent with the objective nature of the test.
* Age may not be a determinative factor in every case, but it is a reality courts cannot ignore.

**Reasoning (Dissent - Alito, J.):**

* The decision is inconsistent with a main justification for _Miranda_: the need for a **clear rule** that is easily applied.
* _Miranda_ set down rigid standards, requiring courts to ignore personal characteristics relevant to actual susceptibility to police pressure, in favor of clarity and ease of application.
* _Miranda_'s objective reasonable-person test for custody is a key contributor to its clarity.
* The custody analysis is based on whether a hypothetical **reasonable person** would consider themselves confined.
* Many suspects differ from this hypothetical person, **but vulnerable defendants can still challenge confessions under the constitutional rule against actual coercion.**
* Today's decision shifts the custody determination from a "one-size-fits-all" test to one accounting for at least one individualized characteristic—age—that correlates with susceptibility.
* Age is not the only such characteristic; others like intelligence, education, occupation, or prior experience also correlate with pliability.
* The Court will either have to arbitrarily limit this decision to age or fundamentally transform _Miranda_'s clear prophylactic rule into a highly fact-intensive standard resembling the voluntariness test that _Miranda_ sought to replace.
* There is no need for this change because:
  * **Many minors questioned are near the age of majority, where the one-size-fits-all rule might be a reasonable fit.**
  * Difficulties with minors often arise from the unique circumstances of school interrogations, and the _Miranda_ rule already accounts for the setting.
  * In cases of especially young suspects, courts applying the constitutional **voluntariness standard** can take special care to ensure confessions weren't coerced, protecting minors without changing the _Miranda_ custody rule.
* Safeguarding the rights of minors does not require this **"extreme makeover of&#x20;**_**Miranda**_**".**
