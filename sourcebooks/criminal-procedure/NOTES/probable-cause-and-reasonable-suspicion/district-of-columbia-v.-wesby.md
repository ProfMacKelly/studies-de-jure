# District of Columbia v. Wesby

## Facts

* 1 am - call from a former neighborhood commissioner (i.e., an "advisory neighborhood commissioner," which is a non-partisan elected representative of a designated neighborhood), complaining about loud music and illegal activities in a house that had been vacant for several months.
* District of Columbia police officers responded, and several neighbors confirmed that the house was supposed to be empty.&#x20;
* Officers knocked on the front door, and a man looked out the window and ran upstairs; but someone opened the door, and the officers entered.
* The house was nearly barren and in disarray. The officers smelled marijuana and observed beer bottles and cups of liquor on the floor, which was dirty.&#x20;
  * The floor was so dirty that one of the partygoers refused to sit on it while being questioned. Although the house had working electricity and plumbing, it had no furniture downstairs other than a few padded metal chairs. The only other signs of habitation were blinds on the windows, food in the refrigerator, and toiletries in the bathroom.
  * They found a make-shift strip club in the living room; partially clad women were giving lap dances, people watching were holding cash and alcohol
  * After partygoers scattered, the officers went upstairs and "found more debauchery": A naked woman and several men in an upstairs bedroom.&#x20;
  * A bare mattress (only one in the house) on the floor, candles, open condom wrappers, a used condom on a windowsill,  someone hiding in a closet, and someone who shut himself in the bathroom
* Many partygoers scattered when they saw the uniformed officers, and some hid. The officers found 21 people left and questioned everyone, getting inconsistent stories.&#x20;
* Two women--who were working the party--identified “Peaches” as the house’s tenant and said that she had given the partygoers permission to have the party. But Peaches was not there, and none of the other people knew who invited them or knew Peaches's name. Some people claimed that this was a bachelor party, but no one knew who the bachelor was or whether he was there.
* When the officers spoke by phone to Peaches, she was nervous, agitated, and evasive. At first, she claimed that she was renting the house and had given the partygoers permission to have the party, but she eventually admitted that she did not have permission to use the house.&#x20;
* The owner confirmed that he had not given anyone permission to be there. The officers then arrested the partygoers for unlawful entry.&#x20;
* Several partygoers sued for false arrest under the Fourth Amendment and DC law, and also negligent supervision under DC law. (DC Cir. noted that the officers did not make arrests until instructed to do by their sergeant.)
* The District Court concluded that the officers lacked probable cause to arrest the partygoers for unlawful entry and that two of the officers, petitioners here, were not entitled to qualified immunity.
  * DC Ct: Officers were told that Peaches invited everyone, and nothing else suggested that the people "knew or should have known" that they were trespassing. PC for unlawful entry requires evidence that they knew they didn't have permission to be there
    * Side note: **Because probable cause is an objective standard, an arrest is lawful if the officer had probable cause to arrest for any offense, not just the offense cited at the time of arrest or booking**. See _Devenpeck v. Alford_, [543 U.S. 146](https://casetext.com/case/devenpeck-v-alford-2#p153), 153–155, and n. 2 (2004).
  * DC Circuit: No conflicting info about why they were there (i.e., invited by Peaches or a bachelor party), so no reason to believe they were trespassing; being there for a bachelor party would be consistent with some of the activity

## &#x20;Issue&#x20;

Whether there was PC to arrest the partygoers for unlawful entry

## &#x20;Holding&#x20;

Yes

## &#x20;Rule

* <mark style="background-color:yellow;">Examine the events leading up to the arrest, and then decide</mark> <mark style="background-color:yellow;"></mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**‘whether these historical facts, viewed from the standpoint of an objectively reasonable police officer, amount to’ probable cause.”**</mark>&#x20;

## Analysis

* Because probable cause <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**“deals with probabilities and depends on the totality of the circumstances,” it is “a fluid concept” that is “not readily, or even usefully, reduced to a neat set of legal rules.”**</mark>&#x20;
* PC “<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**requires only a probability or substantial chance of criminal activity, not an actual showing of such activity**</mark>.”&#x20;
* <mark style="background-color:yellow;">Reasonable inferences based on TOC that the partygoers didn't have permission to be there</mark>
  * Caller was the neighborhood commissioner - said house should be vacant
  * On scene, neighbors confirmed the house should have been vacant
  * After knock on door, someone looked out the window and ran upstairs
  * After officers were let into the house, partygoers scattered
  * Homeowners don't live under the circumstances present
  * Inconsistent interview answer
  * Vague and implausible answers
  * Nervous, agitated, evasive behavior
  * Some witnesses said Peaches told them she wasn’t a tenant &#x20;
* Circumstances "susceptible of innocent explanation" is not determinative
* Although **each fact “in isolation," may not have created PC, totality of the circumstances did**
