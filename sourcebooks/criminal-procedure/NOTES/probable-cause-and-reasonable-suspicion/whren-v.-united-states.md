# Whren v. United States

## Facts

* June evening, plainclothes vice-squad officers of the District of Columbia Metropolitan Police Department were patrolling a "**high drug area"** of the city in an unmarked car



* Passed a dark Pathfinder truck with **temporary license plates** and youthful occupants waiting at a stop sign, the **driver looking down into the lap of the passenger** at his right

&#x20;

* The truck remained stopped at the intersection for what seemed an unusually long time-**more than 20 seconds**.

&#x20;

* When the police car executed a U-turn in order to head back toward the truck, the Pathfinder **turned suddenly to its right, without signaling, and sped off at an "unreasonable" speed.**

&#x20;

* Shortly, the police overtook the Pathfinder when it stopped behind other traffic at a red light. They pulled up alongside, and Officer Soto stepped out and approached the driver's door, identifying himself as a police officer and directing the driver, petitioner Brown, to put the vehicle in park.

&#x20;

* When at the driver's side window, Soto immediately observed **two large plastic bags of what appeared to be crack cocaine** in Whren's hands. Petitioners were arrested, and quantities of several types of illegal drugs were retrieved from the vehicle.&#x20;

## Issue

\[**Constitutionality of pretextual stops**:] **Whether it is constitutional to make a stop when there is PC to do so when the officer's true motivation for the stop was unrelated to the traffic stop (i.e., when the officer has an ulterior motive)?**

* (Alternatively: Does it matter whether a reasonable officer would have made the stop for the offense?)
* Also a specific concern over race being a factor in the officer's motivations

## Holding

* Pretextual stops are permitted

## Rule

* **Detention of a motorist is reasonable where probable cause exists to believe that a traffic violation has occurred**
* **Objective reasonableness is the standard for probable cause**
*   Narrow exceptions for **balancing** of 4th Amend interests **(public interests in traffic safety vs. private interests in pretextual stops vs. private interests in motorists understanding what to expect)** when probable cause exists has been limited to:

    * Searches or seizures conducted in an extraordinary manner&#x20;
    * Acts that are unusually harmful to an individual’s privacy or

    **Balancing factors:** Physical interests—such as, for example, seizure by means of deadly force, unannounced entry into a home, entry into a home without a warrant, or physical penetration of the body.
*

## &#x20;**Analysis**

**Petitioner's Arg: No justification to believe they were engaged in illegal drug-related activities** - the "traffic violation" justification was pretext for investigating drug-based activity (i.e., justification for a stop should be based on the LEO's motivation for the stop.)

* \[**Could always find a reason to stop, so motive should matter**]: Automobiles are so heavily regulated that there would be some violation for an officer to catch - encourages officers to take actions other officers in the same circumstances wouldn't make if they didn't have an ulterior motive

**\[COURT]:** Test has always been objective:

* Subjective test is **impractical** — considering what the collective consciousness of law enforcement - different departments have their own policies and practices is **impractical**



* **As for the multitude of traffic regulations**, (1) there is no judicial authority to categorize/measure the violations, and (2) even if there was, there wouldn't be a way to do it that wouldn't be arbitrary



* **Court's Note on Race**: "selective enforcement of the law based on considerations such as race. But the constitutional basis for objecting to intentionally discriminatory application of laws is the Equal Protection Clause, not the Fourth Amendment. Subjective intentions play no role in ordinary, probable-cause Fourth Amendment analysis.
  * Note of Interest - The 14 Amend applies to states, not the federal government, right? But DC is a federal jurisdiction. (Solution: Reverse Incorporation - _Bolling v. Sharpe_ (1954) (no segregation in public schools - this was in DC)
