# New York v. Belton

(6-3 decision)

**Facts**

* New York State Trooper, Douglas W. Nicot, was patrolling the New York State Thruway when he observed a car speeding. The vehicle, a two-door Pontiac, was occupied by four young men: the driver and three passengers, including the respondent, **Roger Belton**.
* Trooper Nicot stopped the car for the traffic violation. As he approached the vehicle, he noticed the smell of burnt marijuana coming from inside. He also observed an envelope on the car floor with the word “**Supergold**” written on it — a term the officer associated with marijuana packaging.
* Based on these observations, Trooper Nicot had probable cause to believe that the occupants had been smoking marijuana in the car. He ordered all four men out of the vehicle and placed them under arrest for **unlawful possession of marijuana** under New York law.
* After arresting the men, the officer **separated them** and ensured they were not within reaching distance of the car. He had no backup and was alone on the scene, so he conducted a search of the **passenger compartment** of the vehicle to secure any contraband or weapons before the car could be impounded or left unattended.
* During the search, he found a **black leather jacket** belonging to Belton on the **back seat** of the car. The officer picked up the jacket, **unzipped a pocket**, and discovered a small quantity of **cocaine** inside. Belton was then additionally charged with possession of a controlled substance.
* Belton moved to suppress the cocaine evidence, arguing that the search of his jacket was an unconstitutional **warrantless search** not justified as an incident to his arrest. The trial court denied the motion, ruling the search valid under the exception for searches incident to a lawful arrest.

**Procedural History**

* The New York Appellate Division reversed, holding the search exceeded the bounds of a search incident to arrest.
* The New York Court of Appeals affirmed the reversal.
* The State of New York appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court.

**Issue**

Is the warrantless search of the passenger compartment of a vehicle, including containers therein, lawful as a **search incident to the lawful custodial arrest** of its occupants?

**Holding**

**Yes.** The Court held that when a police officer has made a **lawful custodial arrest of the occupant** of an automobile, the officer may, <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**as a contemporaneous**</mark> incident of that arrest, search the passenger compartment of the automobile and the containers found therein.

**Rule**

A lawful custodial arrest justifies the contemporaneous search of the passenger compartment of a vehicle and any containers within it (whether open or closed), without a warrant.

**Reasoning**

The Court emphasized the need for a clear and workable rule to apply in vehicle search cases. The decision in **Chimel v. California** (1969) allowed officers to search the arrestee and the area within the arrestee’s immediate control to prevent the destruction of evidence or access to weapons. However, applying Chimel to the automobile context created confusion.

The Court adopted a bright-line rule for vehicles: if a lawful custodial arrest occurs, the entire passenger compartment is deemed within the arrestee’s reach, thus justifying a search. Containers like purses, bags, and jackets fall under this rule.

This approach simplifies the law for officers in the field and furthers the goals of officer safety and evidence preservation.

**Dissent**

Justice Brennan dissented, arguing that the majority **distorted&#x20;**_**Chimel**_**&#x20;by presuming the entire passenger compartment is always within reach of an arrestee.** He emphasized the importance of Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches and the need for case-by-case assessment _**rather than blanket rules**_.

Justice White also dissented, voicing concern over the _**automatic**_ nature of the new rule, which may allow searches that are not truly justified under Chimel.
