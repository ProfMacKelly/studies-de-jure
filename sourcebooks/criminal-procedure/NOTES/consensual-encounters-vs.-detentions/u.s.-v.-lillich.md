## **Facts & Procedural History**

* early morning of February 3, 2019, two officers from the Woodbury County Sheriff's Office—Deputy Michael Lenz and a reserve deputy—were patrolling the small town of Sloan, Iowa, due to break-ins the night before at a church and a school.&#x20;
* At around 2:00 a.m. while driving past a car wash, they **noticed a car in a car wash bay** and decided to observe from a short distance away.&#x20;
* Deputy Lenz was **concerned about a possible burglary, given the break-ins the night before, the time of day, and recent car wash burglaries in nearby counties**.

#### Car Wash&#x20;

* two manual car wash bays that are open and lit 24 hours a day.&#x20;
* Both bays have two garage-type doors, allowing a person to drive in, stop and wash their car, and then drive out.&#x20;
* The car wash owner testified that the **bay doors are kept closed in the winter so the bays stay heated.**&#x20;
  * The south bay (entrance) doors are manual, opened and closed by rope pulleys.&#x20;
  * The north bay (exit) doors open by pressing a button and automatically close when the car drives over a sensor.&#x20;
  * Each bay is accessible from the other by a door between the two bays.

<mark style="background-color:orange;">The</mark> <mark style="color:$danger;background-color:orange;">**i**</mark><mark style="color:$danger;background-color:orange;">**nitial encounter**</mark> is composed of the <mark style="background-color:orange;">seven-minute interval from when officers first entered the car wash bay occupied by Lillich and Steffens to when Deputy Lenz returned their identification cards.</mark>&#x20;

Lillich objected to the ... conclusions that the initial encounter was supported by reasonable suspicion, that the officers were not required to give him Miranda warnings during the first encounter, and that the inevitable discovery doctrine saved from suppression the evidence the officers obtained from his car. The district court \[contrary to the magistrate judge, concluded] that Lillich's initial encounter with police was consensual, but even if it were not, it was supported by reasonable suspicion. The district court adopted the balance of the \[magistrate judge's findings] and granted in part and denied in part Lillich's motion to suppress.

Before trial, Lillich entered a conditional guilty plea on Count 2. In his plea agreement, Lillich reserved the right to appeal the partial denial of his motion to suppress. The district court accepted Lillich's guilty plea and sentenced him to 262 months imprisonment. Lillich now appeals the partial denial of his motion to suppress.

## II. \[Discussion]

<mark style="color:$danger;">Lillich</mark> challenges the district court's partial denial of his motion to suppress, <mark style="color:red;background-color:yellow;">**arguing: (1) the initial encounter was neither consensual nor supported by reasonable suspicion; (2) the initial encounter violated his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination because the encounter was custodial and the officers failed to give him Miranda warnings; and (3) the inevitable discovery doctrine does not apply to save from suppression the evidence found in Lillich's car.**</mark> The **government does not challenge the district court's determination that the second encounter was unlawful or the suppression of Lillich's statements after the second encounter**. \[...]

### **A.** \[Nature of the Encounter]

#### Initial

Lillich first contends that his **initial encounter** with the officers violated the Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable seizures. He **argues that the encounter was neither consensual nor supported by reasonable suspicion**, and thus the district court should have suppressed all evidence the officers obtained as "fruits of the violation of Mr. Lillich's Fourth Amendment right." \[...] Lillich does not challenge any of the district court's factual findings.&#x20;

Thus, <mark style="color:red;background-color:green;">**we consider the purely legal question of whether the initial encounter was either consensual or supported by reasonable suspicion**</mark><mark style="background-color:green;">**.**</mark>

A. "The Fourth Amendment prohibits \`unreasonable searches and seizures' by the Government, a<mark style="background-color:yellow;">nd</mark> <mark style="color:$danger;background-color:yellow;">its protections extend to brief investigatory stops of persons or vehicles that fall short of traditional arrest.</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">"</mark> _United States v. Arvizu_.&#x20;

* <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**A.1 Government officials, however, "do not violate the Fourth Amendment's prohibition of unreasonable seizures**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:$danger;background-color:yellow;">**merely by approaching individuals on the street or in other public places and putting questions to them if they are willing to listen**</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**.**</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">"</mark> [_United States v. Drayton_](#user-content-fn-1)[^1].&#x20;

<mark style="background-color:red;">**B. "\[A] person has been \`seized' within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment only if, in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:red;background-color:red;">**reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave**</mark><mark style="background-color:red;">**.**</mark>" [_United States v. Mendenhall_](#user-content-fn-2)[^2].&#x20;

*   B.1 In certain circumstances, a citizen's freedom <mark style="color:$danger;">**of movement may be "restricted by a factor independent of police conduc**</mark>t," ..., including "by his being a passenger on a bus," ..., or by his being at his workplace. \[...] In those circumstances,&#x20;

    * **"t**<mark style="background-color:green;">**he \`free to leave' analysis ... is inapplicable," and "the appropriate inquiry is whether a reasonable person would feel free to decline the officers' requests or otherwise terminate the encounter."**</mark> \[...]&#x20;

    B2. (Otherwise) Ultimately, a <mark style="color:red;">seizure occurs "when \[an] officer, by means of physical force or show of authority, has in some way restrained the liberty of a citizen.</mark>" \[...]&#x20;

C. W<mark style="background-color:blue;">**ith some exceptions, "both the \`primary evidence obtained as a direct result of an illegal search or seizure' and ... \`evidence later discovered and found to be derivative of an illegality,' the so-called \`fruit of the poisonous tree,'" is inadmissible under the exclusionary rule. \[...]**</mark>

**D. To&#x20;**<mark style="background-color:red;">**determine whether a police-citizen**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:red;background-color:red;">**encounter is consensua**</mark><mark style="color:red;">**l or implicates Fourth Amendment protections**</mark>**, we must "**<mark style="color:$danger;">**consider\[ ] the totality of the circumstances" and "the unique facts of each case.**</mark>**" \[...]. "The test is necessarily imprecise, because it is designed to assess the&#x20;**<mark style="color:$danger;">**coercive effect of police conduct,**</mark>**&#x20;taken as a whole, rather than to focus on particular details of that conduct in isolation."** [_**Michigan v. Chesternut**_](#user-content-fn-3)[^3]**. Several factors inform our analysis, including:**

> <mark style="color:red;">**officers positioning themselves in a way to limit the person's freedom of movement, the presence of several officers, the display of weapons by officers, physical touching, the use of language or intonation indicating compliance is necessary, the officer's retention of the person's property, or an officer's indication the person is the focus of a particular investigation.**</mark>

[_United States v. Griffith_](#user-content-fn-4)[^4]_._

Lillich <mark style="background-color:purple;">**argues that he was seized because his freedom of movement was limited "to a small car wash bay with the exits blocked," and because he was "cornered in a closed area by the officers when the officer's \[sic] \`requested' his license."**</mark> \[...] **We disagree**.&#x20;

<mark style="color:$primary;">**First**</mark>, we note that the magistrate judge found—and Lillich has never contested— "that t<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**he officers asked to see \[Lillich's] identification without indicating it was required, and \[Lillich] voluntarily gave \[his] identification card\[ ] to the officers upon request." \[...] "A request to see identification is not a seizure, \`as long as the police do not convey a message that compliance with their request\[ ] is required.'"**</mark> \[...]&#x20;

1. Additionally, the district court found that, <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**because the officers parked their car in front of the bay**</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**&#x20;**</mark>_<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**next**</mark>_<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**&#x20;**</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**to the bay containing Lillich's car, the officers "did not block an exit \[Lillich and Steffens] could have used if they attempted to terminate the encounter and drive away**</mark>." \[...]
   1. Although Lillich's movements were "<mark style="color:$danger;">limited" in a sense, this was the result of his decisions to wash his car and close both bay doors while doing so</mark><mark style="color:$danger;">**; "it says nothing about whether or not the police conduct at issue was coercive**</mark>**.**" \[...]&#x20;
   2. Further, Lillich <mark style="color:red;">**was "generally unhindered from walking around the bay or continuing to work on the car"**</mark> after the officers arrived. \[...]&#x20;
2. Finally, the <mark style="color:red;">fact that Deputy Simoni</mark> <mark style="color:red;"></mark><mark style="color:red;">**remained near the doorway between the two bays did not meaningfully limit Lillich's freedom**</mark> of movement. Even if the doorway were blocked, and even considering Lillich's legal inability to drive, <mark style="color:$danger;background-color:yellow;">**Lillich had other ways to terminate the encounter. For example, he could have opened the bay door(s) and exited on foot**</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**.**</mark> Accordingly, we find that any limitation the officers placed on Lillich's freedom of movement is <mark style="color:$danger;">**minimal**</mark>, and we give it little weight.

<mark style="color:$primary;">**Additionally**</mark>, we find that <mark style="background-color:red;">**the officers' seven-minute retention of Lillich's identification card was not inconsistent with the type of brief examination Lillich consented to by voluntarily turning over his identification card.**</mark> Moreover, Lillich never requested that the officers return his identification. See [_United States v. Carpenter_](#user-content-fn-5)[^5]. Moreover, Lillich continued to dry off the car while the officers retained his identification, suggesting that the retention of his identification did not interfere with his activities or meaningfully prevent him from terminating the encounter or leaving. The totality of the circumstances indicates that the officers' retention of Lillich's identification did not restrain his liberty. Accordingly, we assign the "retention of property" factor little weight.

<mark style="color:$primary;">**The other circumstances**</mark>**&#x20;support the conclusion that the initial encounter was consensual**.&#x20;

* <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**officers did not activate their patrol car's lights or siren, physically touch Lillich, brandish their weapons, or threaten arrest.**</mark>[<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**\***</mark> ](#user-content-fn-6)[^6]
* <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**The officers' weapons were holstered and visible, and Lillich does not argue that the presence of weapons impacts the analysis.**</mark>&#x20;
  * (Even if he had, this fact does not compel a finding that the encounter was a seizure.[\* ](#user-content-fn-7)[^7])
* The <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**presence of three officers weighs in favor of finding a seizure**</mark>[<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**\***</mark>](#user-content-fn-8)[^8]<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**,**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:$danger;background-color:yellow;">**but**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**it does not outweigh the other facts indicating that no seizure occurred**</mark>.\*[^9]&#x20;
  * The record does <mark style="color:red;background-color:yellow;">**not demonstrate that the officers restrained Lillich's liberty "by means of physical force or show of authority**</mark>."\*[^10] &#x20;
    * <mark style="background-color:green;">**Based on the totality of the circumstances, we conclude that the officers' conduct would not have "communicated to a reasonable person that the person was not free to decline the officers' requests or otherwise terminate the encounter.**</mark>"\*[^11]&#x20;
* Accordingly, we find that <mark style="color:$primary;">**the initial encounter between Lillich and the officers was consensual,**</mark> and thus Lillich's Fourth Amendment rights were not violated.\*[^12]

### B. \[Lack of _Miranda_ Warnings]

Lillich also contends that the district court should have suppressed his statements made during the initial encounter because it was a custodial interrogation requiring Miranda warnings, which the officers failed to give. Again, we disagree.&#x20;

* <mark style="background-color:yellow;">"</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**A consensual encounter does not amount to a custodial situation requiring the administration of Miranda warnings.**</mark>"[\* ](#user-content-fn-13)[^13]
* Because we conclude that the initial encounter was consensual, <mark style="color:$primary;">**we necessarily conclude that the officers were not required to give Lillich Miranda warnings**</mark>**.** Accordingly, the officers did not violate Lillich's Fifth Amendment rights during the first encounter, and the district court did not err in denying Lillich's motion to suppress the statements.

### C. \[Dog Sniff and the Search of the Car]

* Finally, Lillich argues that the drugs and cell phone seized from his car should have been suppressed because they were the fruits of his unlawful seizure. <mark style="background-color:yellow;">Lillich</mark> <mark style="background-color:yellow;"></mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**contends that his car was unlawfully seized to await a drug dog sniff**</mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">,</mark> <mark style="background-color:yellow;">which eventually led the officers to search the car and</mark> <mark style="background-color:yellow;"></mark><mark style="background-color:yellow;">**seize drugs and his cell phone from it**</mark>. Lillich does not contest that the drug dog's positive alert gave the officers probable cause to search the car.\*[^14]

The district court found that the officers lacked reasonable suspicion to detain Lillich in the second encounter, and therefore Lillich was unlawfully seized. However, the d**istrict court ruled that the evidence seized from Lillich's car should not be suppressed because it would have been inevitably discovered during the officers' investigation of Steffens.**

* The <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**"inevitable discovery" doctrine, an exception to the exclusionary rule, "allows for the admission of evidence that would have been discovered even without the unconstitutional source."**</mark>[<mark style="background-color:yellow;">**\***</mark> ](#user-content-fn-15)[^15]The **district court** found that the officers had reasonable suspicion to seize Lillich's car for a drug dog sniff because&#x20;
  * "**\[t]he drugs found on Steffens, combined with his potential outstanding warrant for \`dangerous drugs' and the factors that led to the initial encounter, created reasonable suspicion that Lillich's \[car] might contain further evidence of drugs or drug-related crimes.**" \[...]&#x20;

The dis**trict court further reasoned** that because Steffens was the driver, there was a sufficient nexus between him and Lillich's car <mark style="background-color:yellow;">**"because in most cases drivers exercise sufficient dominion and control" over a car to justify further searches for drugs**</mark>. \[...][^16] Additionally, the district court found that <mark style="background-color:yellow;">Lillich would not have been able to take his car after he asked to leave, since he was legally unable to drive, and even if he had exited on foot, he would not have been able to get his phone out of his car before leaving, since less than a minute elapsed from the time he asked to leave and when the officers found drugs on Steffens.</mark>

We conclude that the district court did not err in denying the motion to suppress the evidence found in Lillich's car, albeit for a different reason. \[...]&#x20;

* We agree with <mark style="color:$danger;background-color:yellow;">**the government that the district court did not need to rely on the inevitable discovery doctrine because the drugs seized from Lillich's car were**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:$danger;background-color:red;">**not**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:$danger;background-color:yellow;">**the fruit of Lillich's unlawful detention.**</mark>
  * <mark style="background-color:green;">**Evidence should be excluded only "**</mark><mark style="color:$danger;background-color:green;">**if the \`illegality is at least a but-for cause of obtaining the evidence.**</mark><mark style="background-color:green;">**'"**</mark>[<mark style="background-color:green;">**\***</mark> ](#user-content-fn-17)[^17]<mark style="background-color:green;">**Lillich bears the burden of establishing a nexus between the constitutional violation and the discovery of the drugs and cell phone.**</mark>[<mark style="background-color:green;">**\***</mark>](#user-content-fn-18)[^18]
    * Lillich has failed to demonstrate the requisite nexus between his detention and the discovery of the evidence. <mark style="color:red;background-color:green;">**No evidence from Lillich's person, after he asked to leave, was used to support the seizure or search of the car**</mark>.&#x20;
      * For the less <mark style="color:red;">**than one minute between when Lillich asked to leave and when the officers found drugs on Steffens, Lillich's car was stationary because Lillich legally was unable to drive**</mark>**&#x20;it and&#x20;**<mark style="color:red;">**Steffens, the driver, was under arrest**</mark>**.**&#x20;
        * <mark style="background-color:green;">The detention of</mark> <mark style="background-color:green;"></mark><mark style="background-color:green;">**Lillich's person had**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:red;background-color:green;">**nothing to do with the car remaining stationary for that one-minute period**</mark><mark style="background-color:green;">.</mark>&#x20;
      * Thereafter, the <mark style="color:red;background-color:green;">**evidence relied upon to support the officers' seizure of the car to await the drug dog came from Steffens: the drugs found in Steffens's pocket and Steffens's outstanding warrant for "dangerous drugs."**</mark> <mark style="color:red;">A</mark>nd Lillich told the officers during the initial encounter that he was barred from driving and that Steffens was driving the car.&#x20;
      * Lillich challenges the district court's conclusion that the drugs found on Steffens provided reasonable suspicion to seize the car to await a drug dog sniff. He argues that there was an insufficient nexus between Steffens and Lillich's car: although Steffens was the driver, "it still is not clear from the record what other facts show he had dominion or control over the vehicle." \[...] <mark style="color:red;background-color:green;">**But the presence of drugs in Steffens's—the driver's—pocket provided reasonable suspicion to seize the car to await a drug dog sniff.**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="background-color:green;">**\[...**</mark>] No "other facts" were needed. Therefore, we reject Lillich's argument that the officers lacked authority to seize Lillich's car.&#x20;
      * Further, as stated above, Lillich does not contest that the drug dog's positive alert on his car gave the officers probable cause to search it. Lillich's unlawful detention "did not contribute in any way to discovery of the evidence" in Lillich's car\*[^19], and therefore Lillich <mark style="color:red;">**has not demonstrated that the "illegality is ... a but-for cause of obtaining the evidence."**</mark>[<mark style="color:red;">**\***</mark> ](#user-content-fn-20)[^20]Accordingly, the district court did not err in denying Lillich's motion to suppress the drugs and cell phone seized from his car.
* Lillich argues that the district court's analysis erroneously assumed that his cell phone was "always going to stay in the \[car] and always going to be searched." \[...]&#x20;
  * <mark style="background-color:green;">**He**</mark>**&#x20;**<mark style="color:$danger;background-color:green;">**implies that if he had been permitted to leave the second encounter when he requested, he would have taken the phone with him**</mark><mark style="background-color:green;">**, preventing the officers from seizing it from the car.**</mark>&#x20;
    * <mark style="background-color:green;">**But the district court found that Lillich factually could not have left with his phone before reasonable suspicion arose to seize his car (which contained his phone) because less than one minute elapsed between his request to leave and the officers' discovery of drugs on Steffens**</mark>. Lillich does not explain why this finding was erroneous. And the record shows that Deputy Simoni later looked for Lillich's phone in the car but could not find it, suggesting that the phone was not immediately accessible and that it would have taken a minute or longer to retrieve the phone and leave with it. Thus, Lillich has not demonstrated that the district court's factual finding is clearly erroneous.\*[^21]&#x20;

\[...]

We affirm the district court's judgment.
