# U.S. v. Arvizu

**FACTS**

* A Border Patrol Agent received information that a vehicle sensor had been triggered in a remote area. The agent suspected that the vehicle could be attempted to evade a checkpoint as the timing corresponded with a shift change, leaving the area unpatrolled. The agent located the vehicle, a minivan.&#x20;
* Agent obtained a visual vantage point by pulling off to the side of the road at an angle so he could see the oncoming vehicle as it passed by. The agent observed:
  * the vehicle slow considerably as it approached his position
  * the driver appeared stiff and rigid
  * the driver seemed to pretend the agent was not there
  * the knees of the child passengers in the very back seat were unusually high (as if their feet were elevated by something on the floor).
* After following the vehicle for a short distance, agent observed:
  * the children, while facing forward, wave at the agent in an abnormal fashion
  * the strange waving continued intermittently for four to five minutes
  * the driver signaled for a turn, turned the signal off, then suddenly signaled and turned the vehicle
  * the turn was the last that would allow the vehicle to avoid the checkpoint
  * the road is rough and usually utilized by four-wheel-drive vehicles
  * the vehicle did not appear to be part of the local traffic
  * there were no recreation areas associated with this road&#x20;
* Agent requested vehicle registration information via the radio&#x20;
* Learned that the vehicle was registered to an address four blocks north of the border in an area known for alien and narcotics smuggling&#x20;
* At this point, the agent decided to conduct a traffic stop&#x20;

**ISSUE:**    **Whether the agent could articulate reasonable suspicion to conduct a Terry stop considering all observed factors had innocent explanations?**

&#x20;**HELD:** Yes

&#x20;DISCUSSION:&#x20;

* **Balance between the public interest and the individual's right to personal security**: Tilts in favor of a standard less than probable cause in brief investigatory stops of persons or vehicles&#x20;
* Fourth Amendment is satisfied if the officer's action is supported by reasonable suspicion to believe that criminal activity "may be afoot"&#x20;
* “When discussing how reviewing courts should make reasonable-suspicion determinations, we have said repeatedly that they must look at the **‘totality of the circumstances’ of each case to see whether the detaining officer has a ‘particularized and objective basis’ for suspecting legal wrongdoing.**” In doing so, it is imperative that the officer be allowed to use “**their own experience and specialized training to make inferences”** about a circumstance.&#x20;
* Circumstances:&#x20;
  * Registration check
  * Experience relating to the route
  * Timing - shift change
  * Reactions of occupants

&#x20;Otherwise **innocent actions, considered together**, may warrant a further look by a law enforcement officer.

&#x20;\---------------

The Supreme Court determined that the border patrol agent's actions were reasonable based on the totality of the circumstances, considering several factors:

Suspicious behavior:

* Route: The minivan was traveling on a remote, unpaved road rarely used except by smugglers to avoid the checkpoint.

&#x20;

* Timing: The timing coincided with the shift change when backroad patrols were unpatrolled, creating an opportunity for smuggling.

&#x20;

* Driving: The driver slowed down significantly, appeared stiff, and didn't acknowledge the agent, which could be unusual in that rural area.

&#x20;

* Children: The children's elevated knees suggested concealed cargo, and their synchronized, prolonged waving seemed suspicious.

&#x20;

* Turnoff: The minivan abruptly turned onto a rougher road rarely used by local traffic, further indicating an attempt to avoid the checkpoint.

Agent's experience and knowledge:

* The agent knew this area was used by smugglers and was familiar with local customs.
* The registration check revealed the minivan originated from an area known for smuggling.

Corroborating factors:

* The existence of picnic grounds in other directions made the chosen route less likely for a recreational trip.
* Taking the main road would have been easier for a legitimate outing.

Reasonable suspicion standard:

* While each factor might have an innocent explanation, taken together, they created a particularized and objective basis for suspicion, exceeding the "hunch" standard but not requiring absolute certainty.

Conclusion:

Considering all the above, the Supreme Court found that the agent had reasonable suspicion to believe criminal activity was afoot, justifying the stop and subsequent search that uncovered the marijuana.

Important points:

* The court emphasized the totality of the circumstances approach, considering all factors in context.
* An officer's experience and knowledge of the area can be crucial in interpreting suspicious behavior.
* Reasonable suspicion doesn't require certainty, but it's more than just a hunch.
