# Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court of Nevada

### FACTS

A police officer responded to a call reporting that a man assaulted a woman. The officer found Defendant Larry Hiibel standing outside a parked truck with a woman inside the truck. The officer asked for Defendant's identification 11 times and was refused each time. The officer arrested Defendant. Defendant was convicted for "willfully resist\[ing], delay\[ing] or obstruct\[ing] a public officer" in carrying out his duties under [Nev. Rev. Stat. § 171.123](https://nevada.public.law/statutes/nrs_171.123), a "stop and identify" statute that required Defendant only to disclose his name.&#x20;

&#x20;“1. Any peace officer may detain any person whom the officer encounters under circumstances which reasonably indicate that the person has committed, is committing or is about to commit a crime."

&#x20;“3. The officer may detain the person pursuant to this section only to ascertain his identity and the suspicious circumstances surrounding his presence abroad. Any person so detained shall identify himself, but may not be compelled to answer any other inquiry of any peace officer.”

&#x20;The Nevada Supreme Court has interpreted the instant statute to require only that a suspect disclose his name. It does not require him to produce a driver’s license or any other document. If they choose to either to state his name or communicate it to the officer by other means, the statute is satisfied and no violation occurs.

&#x20;Def also claims the statue violates right against self-incrimination

### &#x20;ISSUE

Was Defendant’s constitutional right violated when he was convicted after he refused to tell the police officer his name?

### &#x20;HELD&#x20;

&#x20;No - States may require a suspect to disclose his name in the course of a Terry stop

### &#x20;RULE

* Reasonable suspicion that a person may be involved in criminal activity permits the officer to stop the person for a brief time and take additional steps to investigate further, including asking for suspect's identity&#x20;
* Terry stop requirements:
  * It must justified at its inception AND
  * Be “reasonably related in scope to the circumstances which justified” the initial stop.

### &#x20;DISCUSSION

4th Amend:

* An identity request has an immediate relation to the Terry stop’s purpose, rationale, and practical demands, and the threat of criminal sanction helps ensure that the request does not become a legal nullity.&#x20;
* She statute does not alter the nature of the stop itself, changing neither its duration nor its location. &#x20;

5th Amend:

* 5th Amend protects against:
  * Compelled testimony AND Disclosures that the witness reasonably believes could be used in a criminal prosecution or could lead to other evidence that might be so used&#x20;
* Disclosure of name/identity did not pose a reasonable danger of self-incrimination&#x20;
* Identifying oneself would pose such a danger only in "unusual circumstances"
  * E.g.: Where there is a substantial allegation that furnishing identity at the time of a stop would have given the police a link in the chain of evidence needed to convict the individual of a separate offense

&#x20;1\. Reasonable suspicion for the stop: The officer had received a report of an assault and observed skid marks near the vehicle, indicating a possible connection to the incident. This justified the initial stop under the Terry v. Ohio standard.

&#x20;2\. Limited scope of the request: The officer only asked for identification, which is a routine and accepted part of Terry stops. He did not demand any further information beyond the suspect's name.

&#x20;3\. Legitimate government interests: Knowing the suspect's identity helps assess the situation, identify potential threats, and clear individuals not involved. This is particularly important in domestic violence cases.

&#x20;4\. Balancing test under the Fourth Amendment: The minimal intrusion of asking for a name was balanced against the legitimate government interests, demonstrating the action's reasonableness.

&#x20;5\. State law supporting the request: Nevada's "stop and identify" statute further solidified the officer's right to request identification within the context of a valid Terry stop.

&#x20;6\. Limited consequences: Refusal to comply only resulted in arrest and a small fine, not additional charges or prolonged detention.

### &#x20;DISSENT

1\. Precedent and consistency: They argued that the Court's prior rulings in Terry v. Ohio, Brown v. Texas, and Berkemer v. McCarty established a clear precedent: **during a Terry stop, individuals are not obligated to answer questions and cannot be compelled to do so.** The majority's decision contradicted this established legal principle.&#x20;

* Although Berkemer v. McCarty language was dicta it is generally taken as a statement of law and there is no good reason to reject it:&#x20;
  * “officer may ask the \[Terry] detainee a moderate number of questions to determine his identity and to try to obtain information confirming or dispelling the officer’s suspicions. But the detainee is not obliged to respond.”&#x20;

2\. Lack of compelling justification: The dissent criticized the majority for failing to demonstrate a significant need for changing the existing rule. They argued that the majority did not provide evidence that the previous rule significantly hindered law enforcement or presented any other convincing reason for deviating from it.

&#x20;3\. Erosion of a clear rule: The dissent emphasized the importance of maintaining clear and consistent legal principles. They expressed concern that the majority's decision would create confusion and uncertainty by creating an exception to a well-established rule, potentially leading to its further erosion.

&#x20;4\. Implications for individual rights: The dissenting justices highlighted the potential impact on individual rights. They argued that allowing police to compel identification during Terry stops could create a slippery slope, infringing upon individuals' right to remain silent and potentially leading to further intrusions.

After having identified himself or herself as a law enforcement officer, a law enforcement officer may stop a person in a public place for a reasonable period of time when the officer reasonably suspects that such person is committing, is about to commit or has committed a crime, and may demand the name and address of the person and an explanation of the person's conduct. Such detention and temporary questioning shall be conducted in the vicinity where the person was stopped.
