# U.S. v. Place

**Significance:**

* _United States v. Place_ clarified the circumstances under which law enforcement can detain luggage for investigative purposes.
* It established the **"dog sniff" exception** to the Fourth Amendment's search requirement.
* It also placed limits on the **length of time that law enforcement may detain luggage** under the _Terry_ stop type situation.

### **Facts:**

* **Initial Suspicion in Miami:**
  * Raymond Place's behavior at Miami International Airport attracted the attention of law enforcement. His actions were deemed unusual, and discrepancies in his provided information heightened suspicion.
  * Specifically, the officers noted **inconsistencies in the addresses listed on his luggage tags**.
  * Though Place consented to an initial search of his luggage, the imminent departure of his flight prevented the officers from conducting it.
  * The Miami-Dade detectives **relayed their suspicions and information to Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) agents in New York**.
* **Encounter and Seizure in New York:**
  * Upon Place's arrival at LaGuardia Airport in New York City, DEA **agents were waiting**.
  * They approached Place, identified themselves, and requested identification, which he provided.
  * When the agents asked to search his luggage, **Place refused**.
  * The DEA agents t**hen seized his luggage, informing him that they intended to obtain a search warrant**.
  * The agents **transported the luggage to Kennedy Airport, where a narcotics detection dog was available**.
  * A period of approximately **90 minutes elapsed between the seizure of the luggage** at LaGuardia and the dog sniff at Kennedy.
  * The trained narcotics **dog alerted** to the presence of narcotics in one of Place's suitcases.
* **Subsequent Events:**
  * Based on the **dog's alert, the DEA agents obtained a search warrant** for the suitcase.
  * The search revealed cocaine.
  * **Place was arrested and subsequently indicted for possession of cocaine with intent to distribute**.

**Procedural History (More Detailed):**

* **District Court:**
  * Place moved to suppress the evidence (the cocaine), <mark style="background-color:orange;">**arguing that the warrantless seizure of his luggage violated his Fourth Amendment rights**</mark>.
  * The District Court <mark style="background-color:orange;">denied the motion, applying the</mark> <mark style="background-color:orange;"></mark>_<mark style="background-color:orange;">Terry v. Ohio</mark>_ <mark style="background-color:orange;"></mark><mark style="background-color:orange;">standard to the detention of personal property</mark>.&#x20;
  * The **court found that the DEA agents had reasonable suspicion to believe the luggage contained narcotics, justifying the detention**.
  * Place was convicted after entering a guilty plea, reserving his right to appeal the suppression issue.
* **Court of Appeals:**
  * The Court of Appeals reversed the District Court's decision.
  * It <mark style="background-color:orange;">**held that the prolonged seizure of Place's luggage exceeded the permissible limits of a**</mark><mark style="background-color:orange;">**&#x20;**</mark>_<mark style="background-color:orange;">**Terry**</mark>_<mark style="background-color:orange;">**-type investigative stop, thus constituting a seizure without probable cause in violation of the Fourth Amendment.**</mark>

**Key Legal Principles and Reasoning (Expanded):**

* **Application of&#x20;**_**Terry v. Ohio**_**:**
  * The Court considered whether the principles established in _<mark style="background-color:blue;">**Terry v. Ohio**</mark>_<mark style="background-color:blue;">**&#x20;**</mark><mark style="background-color:blue;">**(which allows for brief investigative stops of persons based on reasonable suspicion) could be extended to the detention of personal property**</mark><mark style="background-color:blue;">.</mark>
  * The Court concluded that, under <mark style="background-color:red;">**certain circumstances, law enforcement officers could seize luggage for brief investigative purposes based on reasonable suspicion**</mark><mark style="background-color:red;">.</mark>
* **Reasonable Suspicion vs. Probable Cause:**
  * The Court emphasized the distinction between reasonable suspicion and probable cause.
  * <mark style="background-color:red;">**Reasonable suspicion allows for a brief investigatory detention, while probable cause is required for a full-scale search or arrest.**</mark>
*   **Limits on Investigative Detention:**

    * The Court stressed that investigative detentions of luggage must be <mark style="background-color:red;">**limited in scope and duration.**</mark>
    * The **90-minute detention of Place's luggage was deemed excessive,** particularly because of the DEA agents' lack of diligence and failure to adequately inform Place about the detention.
    * With luggage, there is a possessory interest AND a liberty interest

    * when the police seize luggage from the suspect’s custody, we think the limitations applicable to investigative detentions of the person should define the permissible scope of an investigative detention of the person’s luggage on less than probable cause.&#x20;
    * Under this standard, it is clear that the police conduct here exceeded the permissible limits of a Terry-type investigative stop.

* **Dog Sniff as a "Search":**
  * A significant aspect of the case was the Court's determination that a <mark style="background-color:red;">**"sniff test" by a well-trained narcotics detection dog does not constitute a "search"**</mark> <mark style="background-color:red;"></mark><mark style="background-color:red;">under the Fourth Amendment.</mark>
  * The Court reasoned that a dog sniff is a <mark style="background-color:red;">**limited intrusion that reveals only the presence or absence of contraband**</mark><mark style="background-color:red;">.</mark>
