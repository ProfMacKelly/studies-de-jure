# U.S. v. Sokolow

### FACTS

* Month of July: At Honolulu Airport, Def purchased two round-trip tickets for a flight to Miami leaving later that day&#x20;
* The tickets were purchased in the names of "Andrew Kray" and "Janet Norian," and had open return dates&#x20;
* Def paid $2,100 for the tickets from a large roll of $20 bills, which appeared to contain a total of $4,000&#x20;
* Def also gave the ticket agent his home telephone number&#x20;
* Ticket agent noticed that respondent seemed nervous; he was about 25 years old; he was dressed in a black jumpsuit and wore gold jewelry; and he was accompanied by a woman&#x20;
* Neither Def nor his companion checked any of their four pieces of luggage&#x20;
* Then, ticket agent told Honolulu police offer about the cash purchase&#x20;
* Officer learned:
  * Phone number was listed to "Karl Herman," and he couldn't find a listing for "Andrew Kray" (although this wasn't illegal at the time)
  * &#x20;Return reservations from Miami were for 3 days after leaving Honolulu
  * At LA stopover, Def "he appeared very nervous and was looking around the waiting area"&#x20;
* When Def arrived in Honolulu:
  * Still hadn't checked luggage
  * Wearing same black jumpsuit and gold jewelry&#x20;
* DEA agents approached while Def hailing a cab and asked for his ticket and ID; said he didn't have either, said his name was "Sokolow" and was traveling under mother's maiden name
* Taken to DEA office at airport. Dog alerted to shoulder bag and agents arrested Def. After obtaining a search warrant for the bag, no illegal drugs were found&#x20;
* Dog reexamined the luggage and alerted on another bag; too late in evening to get another warrant, so kept bag but allowed Def to leave for the evening. In morning, a second dog alerted on the bag, another warrant was obtained and cocaine was discovered.&#x20;
* Miami was a common source for illegal drugs&#x20;
* Would be uncommon for Honolulu residents to travel for 20 hours to spend 48 hours in Miami during July&#x20;

### ISSUE

Whether the DEA agents who stopped the defendant had reasonable suspicion that he was involved in criminal activity at the time of the stop?

### HOLDING

Yes. Based on the totality of the circumstances known to the agents at the time of the stop, they had a reasonable suspicion that criminal activity was afoot.

### RULE

TOC;

Reasonableness does not necessarily depend on whether police used the least intrusive means

### REASONING

* In determining the legality of a Terry stop, the totality of the circumstances is considered.
  * The travel pattern wasn't enough
  * Alias wasn't enough
  * Paying in cash (from a roll of $20s) wasn't enough
  * Same clothes wasn't enough&#x20;
* None of the factors known to the agents at the time of the stop, standing alone, was proof of illegal activity. However, when considered together, the facts amounted to reasonable suspicion.&#x20;
* “there could, of course, be circumstances in which wholly lawful conduct might justify the suspicion that criminal activity was afoot.”&#x20;
* Although a profile is general, it doesn't automatically invalidate

### DISSENT

The reasonable suspicion standard is narrow: only to those brief detentions which fall short of being full-scale searches and seizures and which are necessitated by law enforcement exigencies such as the need to stop ongoing crimes, to prevent imminent crimes, and to protect law enforcement officers in highly charged situations.

* intended to protect innocent persons from being subjected to “overbearing or harassing” police conduct carried out solely on the basis of imprecise stereotypes of what criminals look like, or on the basis of irrelevant personal characteristics such as race.
* suspicion is not reasonable unless officers have based it on “specific and articulable facts.”&#x20;
  * It is not enough to suspect that an individual has committed crimes in the past, harbors unconsummated criminal designs, or has the propensity to commit crimes.&#x20;
  * Before detaining an individual, law enforcement officers must reasonably suspect that he is engaged in, or poised to commit, a criminal ac**t&#x20;**_**at that moment**._
* Relying on profiles can dull the officer's ability to make sensitive and fact-sensitive inferences IN LIGHT OF THEIR PERSONAL EXPERIENCE
* Reflexive reliance on a profile of drug courier characteristics runs a far greater risk than does ordinary, case-by-case police work of subjecting innocent individuals to unwarranted police harassment and detention.&#x20;
* This risk is enhanced by the profile’s “chameleon-like way of adapting to any particular set of observations.
